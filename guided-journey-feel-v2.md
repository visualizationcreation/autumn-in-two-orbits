# Autumn, in Two Orbits

## ORB Feel & Experience — Guided Audio Journey

### Where the light goes — an autumn walk

Revised complete text • unrecorded • revision feel-v2

Original companion prose by the primary assistant. This imagined walk connects the twelve existing poem and photograph points. The complete public-domain poems, historical notes, and photo credits remain separately available in the reader.

Let yourself arrive where you are. You might ease into the support beneath you and leave your breathing to its own rhythm. Your eyes can stay open. For a little while, imagine a path through an autumn afternoon: an orchard, a stream, a hillside, a place to rest as evening comes. The photographs offer real glimpses of the season; the path that joins them is a dream made for this journey.

At the orchard gate, a low branch reaches into the light. One apple glows red where the sun touches it. Behind it, the leaves are almost black. There is a scent of damp earth under the trees, and the grass gives slightly beneath your imagined steps. Stand here long enough for the smaller things to appear: a curved stem, a crease in a leaf, the thin shadow one fruit lays across another.

Keats’s autumn is full of ripening and harvest work. Follow the poem’s changing pace: the first abundance, the resting figures, the long wait beside the cider press. Then the view opens into sound. A cricket. A bird. Swallows gathering overhead. Let each have a little distance around it, as though you have stopped walking and the orchard has become easier to hear.

Come closer to the apple that caught the light. In the photograph, its red stands out among deep green leaves. In your imagined orchard, lift an open palm beneath the fruit and feel how a whole season might gather into that small weight. The skin is cool and smooth; the stem holds firm. You leave it on the branch.

For a moment, there is only this nearness: fruit, leaf, hand, the mild air between them. The work of the orchard continues beyond the edge of the scene. Here, you have paused beside one thing growing.

As you lower your hand, another red comes into view farther along the path. A maple leans over the meadow’s edge. Its leaves catch the breeze more readily than the apples do, fluttering like little pieces of cloth. Follow that lighter movement out from under the trees.

The meadow gives you more sky. Under the maple, the path is scattered with leaves, some curled at the edges, others lying open enough to show their fine branching veins. A dry one shifts beneath your foot with a small, papery sound.

In Dickinson’s early published poem, the maple wears a scarf and the field a gown. The speaker notices the season dressing up and decides to put on a trinket. Let that small joke bring you into the company of the landscape. Imagine choosing one fallen leaf for its color alone: a little scarlet, a little gold, one crooked point. Hold it beside the sleeve of your coat.

There is something companionable about this brief poem. Its world has room for an ordinary person making an ordinary gesture. Stay with that scale for a while. A leaf beside a sleeve. A tree moving overhead. The afternoon going on around you, with this small moment of participation tucked inside it.

Look upward through the red canopy. The photograph leaves small openings of sky between the leaves; let your attention rest in one of those pale spaces. Then notice the red around it again. Color, opening, color.

In the meadow of the journey, a breeze passes through the maple. The nearest leaves stir first, and then a whole branch answers. You hear a soft, uneven rustle. The leaf in your hand turns with the air, showing a quieter color underneath.

You set it among the others beside the path. A few steps farther on, the rustling thins, and another sound becomes distinct: water moving over stones. Through the trunks, a quick flash of sunlight appears and vanishes. Follow the sound toward the stream.

At the water’s edge, the afternoon becomes lively again. A little run of current slips between two stones and opens into a shallow pool. The sound is bright at the narrow place, softer where the water spreads. A leaf turns once on the surface, catches briefly against a twig, and moves on.

Dunbar’s speaker meets autumn with a spirited argument: the season can laugh, sing, and dress in splendid colors. Let the playfulness of his poem move beside the stream. There is room here for your own mood, too. If the day has felt heavy, you can sit with that heaviness while the water keeps its quick, uneven music. Delight can be something you encounter at the edge of your attention.

Watch one patch of light. It breaks into several pieces, draws together, and changes again. You might find yourself waiting for the next gleam. The poem’s cheerfulness has this kind of motion—something to spend a little time with, while the leaves and water continue on their way.

Stay beside the stream. In the photograph, sunlight comes through the woodland and reaches the water between dark stones. Let your eye travel from the lit leaves to a patch of moss, then down to the pale movement of the current.

The imagined bank is cool in the shade. You can picture sitting on a dry stone a little back from the water, your hands resting easily. Listen close to the bank, then farther downstream. The separate sounds begin to belong to one moving place.

When you are ready to continue, the path rises gently away from the water. Its sound stays with you for several steps. Then the trees grow farther apart. Light reaches your face again, and through the last branches you see the long, open folds of a hillside.

Beyond the trees, the sky feels very wide. Hills overlap in the distance, their wooded slopes carrying patches of yellow, brown, and lingering green. Close to the path, a grass stem bends and straightens. You have come from watching the smallest turns of water to a view the eye can wander across.

Helen Hunt Jackson’s October is lavish with color, and her sonnet also gives death a place within that splendor. Allow that sharper note to remain in the poem. Perhaps the hillside seems all the more vivid because its colors are passing. Perhaps, today, you simply want to look. Both ways of being here can have their space.

Choose one patch of gold on a distant slope. Let it hold your attention for a few breaths, as naturally as a sound might. Then take in the blue above it. The small detail and the larger view belong to the same afternoon. There is time to move gently between them.

Rest with the photograph’s open hills and sky. A white cloud gives the blue a shape to gather around; below it, the slopes fold into darker hollows. You can let your gaze travel as far as it wishes, then come back to the nearer ground.

In the journey, the afternoon begins to lean toward evening. A shadow lengthens across the path. The grass still catches the sun at its tips, while the earth beneath it has grown cool. Walk a little farther along the slope, carrying the sound of the stream only as a memory now.

Ahead, a thin thread of smoke lifts above a sheltered place. As the light leaves the hillside, a smaller brightness becomes visible near the ground. The path bends toward it.

You reach a place to sit a comfortable distance from a small fire. In this imagined evening, the warmth comes and goes with the air. One side of a stone catches the glow; its other side is already the color of dusk. Farther away, the outlines of the hills become simple and dark.

Stevenson’s short poem follows the red of autumn fires and the rising trail of smoke. Summer’s flowers have finished, and his attention finds another kind of brightness. Let the short lines keep their plain, almost singing rhythm. After the wide hillside, there is something easy to follow in this small scene: a red center, a lifting flame, a gray strand loosening into the sky.

Sit with the changing light for a moment. Imagine the warmth reaching the backs of your hands, then a cooler breath of air passing over them. The scene can be as vivid or as faint as it comes. Here in the room, your body remains supported while the imagined evening gathers around the fire.

The photograph shows two people beside a campfire on a hillside at sunset. Let their presence remain ordinary and unknown; the picture gives you a moment of company without telling you their story.

In your own imagined resting place, there is room beside the warmth. A twig settles with a small click. Smoke drifts sideways for a moment and then rises. You may linger with the sense of sharing a little light as the larger landscape grows dim.

When you stand to continue, the path leads only a short way farther, to a table beneath a shelter. A basket rests near its edge. The yellow of the fruit catches the remaining light. After the restless movement of flame, your attention comes to rest on something still.

At the table, imagine drawing out a chair. The wood is worn smooth along its front edge. There are pears in the basket, and in this dreamed scene you might place a small bunch of grapes beside them, letting Blake’s opening image join the harvest.

In his poem, Autumn becomes a guest invited under a roof to rest and sing. This is Blake’s personification: a season given a voice and a departing figure. Follow it as you would the changing shape of a dream. The guest rises and goes over the hills; the harvest remains.

Set your hands near the basket. The fruit has the quiet particularity of things seen close up: one pear leans against another, a stem bends, a small mark interrupts the yellow skin. The journey began beside an apple still on its branch. Now it has brought you to fruit gathered and set down. Around this table, the day feels spacious enough to include both the guest’s leaving and what has been left to share.

Stay with the pears for another moment. Their colors are soft and uneven, yellow passing into green, a blush on one side. Picture the weight of one resting in your palm. Then place it gently back among the others.

The table, the basket, and the last light can remain here as you return. Perhaps a detail from the walk comes with you: red against dark leaves, a papery rustle, water bright between stones, a cloud above the hills, warmth moving across your hands. Let whichever one appears have a little space. The six poets have offered different ways of meeting the season, and you can leave their voices distinct.

Begin to notice the surface actually supporting you. Feel where your hands are resting, or where your feet meet the floor. Allow an ordinary sound in the room to come forward. If your eyes have been closed, open them when you wish; if they have stayed open, let the room’s edges become clearer.

There is time for one small movement—a shift of your fingers, a change of position, a look toward the window. Let the imagined path recede at its own pace. You are here, with the rest of your day still ahead of you.

1. **keats** — John Keats, *To Autumn*. Text as presented by the Academy of American Poets; written September 19, 1819, first published 1820. Three stanzas of eleven lines. [Poem source](https://poets.org/poem/autumn).

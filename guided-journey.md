# Autumn, in Two Orbits

## ORB Feel & Experience — Guided Audio Journey

Complete guided text • unrecorded • revision feel-v1

This is original companion prose. The poets’ complete, separately attributed poems remain in the reader.

You may settle into a comfortable position, with your eyes open and your breath finding its own pace. Imagine an autumn path beginning just outside an orchard. This is a place made for the journey, not a reconstruction of Keats’s walk. The air is cool near your cheeks, and an apple carries a small weight of water on its skin. Let the first lines arrive as slowly as the fruit has ripened. Behind the sweetness are hands, fields, and the work of harvest. Nothing here has to hurry into winter. As the poem turns toward its smaller sounds, you can leave a little room around each one: a cricket, a bird, the light movement of wings.

Stay a moment in the orchard’s soft light. You might imagine the rough bark close to your hand, and the small coolness of the apple before it warms against your palm. Then let your attention drift past the fruit to a branch beginning to change color. The world is putting on something bright, and another voice is ready to notice.

The orchard opens into a meadow, and you can let the day become a little lighter. A maple branch holds red against the sky. In the early published Dickinson poem, the landscape dresses itself, and the speaker decides to join it. You need not imagine wearing anything elaborate. Perhaps one leaf is enough: a small brightness carried for the pleasure of its color. Let the smile in those last lines have its own space. The season does not ask you to stand outside and admire it. In this poem, you can belong to the scene.

Let the red of the leaves meet the blue beyond them. You can look softly, without needing to name every shade. A breeze in the imagined meadow turns one leaf, briefly showing its paler underside. That small motion draws your attention toward water, where sunlight is already moving in little flashes.

At the brook, the light refuses to stay still. You can follow one small gleam as it breaks apart and returns further downstream. Dunbar’s speaker has no patience with the rule that autumn must be sorrowful. Let that refusal be playful rather than another feeling you have to achieve. If your own day is quieter or heavier, the poem can keep its delight without asking you to imitate it. Around the imagined water, crimson leaves move in the current. For a little while, their motion is enough.

Let the water carry the eye outward. Each quick brightness belongs to a surface already changing. You can leave the stream exactly as it is and follow the path up the bank, where the trees begin to open. Ahead, a broad blue sky makes room for the whole hillside.

You arrive where the hillside opens under blue. The imagined trees carry colors that seem almost too many to keep: amber beside russet, pale gold beside red. Jackson calls October a carnival, but her abundance includes an image of death. You can let both remain. The beauty does not need a promise that it will last, and the ending does not erase the beauty. Let your attention rest on the space between the treetops and the sky. There is no need to gather the whole hillside into memory. One patch of light may be plenty.

Stay with that open blue a little longer. Then imagine the sun lowering until the colors deepen and the spaces between the trees turn cool. A garden wall appears along the path. Beyond it, a small glow gathers the last warmth of the day into a different kind of brightness.

In the imagined garden, the day is turning violet. You can remain comfortably back from the small fire, looking at its red center and the pale line of smoke. Stevenson’s poem lets summer be over and still finds something bright. Perhaps you can receive that as an invitation to notice, without making it a rule about your own life. The warmth belongs to this scene; your own body can stay here, supported where you are. Beyond the wall, the hill grows quiet. A little color remains when the flowers are gone.

Watch the imagined smoke become less distinct as it rises. You do not have to follow it all the way. The garden path continues to a sheltered table, where a basket has been set down. After the moving flame, the stillness of ordinary fruit feels especially gentle.

At the table, you find grapes and pears in the evening light. Blake imagines Autumn as a guest who sings and then leaves his harvest behind. You need not picture an actual person passing through. Let the poem’s figure remain a figure, and let the fruit be something your imagination can almost hold. Around it gather the scenes you have crossed: the orchard’s fullness, a bright leaf, the brook’s movement, an open sky, and a little warmth after sunset. None has to become a lesson. For now, you can simply let them share the same quiet table.

You can leave the basket where it is. There is nothing more to gather before returning. Let the imagined hills grow soft at the edge of your attention, and notice the real surface supporting you. Perhaps feel your feet, or the contact of your hands. Let your eyes find an ordinary color in the room. Autumn has held more than one voice: fullness, play, delight, splendor, warmth, and departure. You can return carrying whichever small detail stayed with you—or simply the ease of having paused.

# Through leaves and light — a Pacific Northwest autumn dream

ORB Feel & Experience — Guided Audio Journey

Original companion script · unrecorded. Optional instrumental music is separate from narration.

This imagined walk draws on real photographs in Seattle and Portland; the six poets are not presented as Pacific Northwest writers.

Let yourself arrive where you are. You might ease into the support beneath you and leave your breathing to its own rhythm. Your eyes can stay open. For a little while, imagine a path through an autumn afternoon: an orchard, a stream, a hillside, a place to rest as evening comes. The photographs offer real autumn leaves in Seattle and Portland. The path that joins them is a dream made for this journey, with room for the orchards, water, and evening warmth of six poems from other places.

At the orchard gate, a low branch reaches into the light. One apple glows red where the sun touches it. Behind it, the leaves are almost black. There is a scent of damp earth under the trees, and the grass gives slightly beneath your imagined steps. Stand here long enough for the smaller things to appear: a curved stem, a crease in a leaf, the thin shadow one fruit lays across another.

Keats’s autumn is full of ripening and harvest work. Follow the poem’s changing pace: the first abundance, the resting figures, the long wait beside the cider press. Then the view opens into sound. A cricket. A bird. Swallows gathering overhead. Let each have a little distance around it, as though you have stopped walking and the orchard has become easier to hear.

Let the imagined orchard soften into the photograph before you. Here the golden canopy belongs to Portland Japanese Garden. Dark branches carry the light outward, and green still gathers beneath them. Give your eyes time to follow one branch until it disappears among the leaves.

In the dream of this walk, you pass beneath that canopy. A little coolness reaches your face. The afternoon has the scent of damp soil and fallen leaves, and each opening between the branches seems to hold a quieter piece of sky. The fruit of Keats’s poem can remain with you as an image of ripeness, while the photograph offers the season through leaves.

Farther along the imagined path, the gold deepens into orange and red. A low maple branch comes within easy view. Follow the fine edges of its leaves toward Dickinson’s small, companionable joke about getting dressed for autumn.

The meadow gives you more sky. Under the maple, the path is scattered with leaves, some curled at the edges, others lying open enough to show their fine branching veins. A dry one shifts beneath your foot with a small, papery sound.

In Dickinson’s early published poem, the maple wears a scarf and the field a gown. The speaker notices the season dressing up and decides to put on a trinket. Let that small joke bring you into the company of the landscape. Imagine choosing one fallen leaf for its color alone: a little scarlet, a little gold, one crooked point. Hold it beside the sleeve of your coat.

There is something companionable about this brief poem. Its world has room for an ordinary person making an ordinary gesture. Stay with that scale for a while. A leaf beside a sleeve. A tree moving overhead. The afternoon going on around you, with this small moment of participation tucked inside it.

Come close to the maple leaves in this Seattle photograph. Their narrow points overlap, orange against scarlet, with small shadows held between them. Choose one edge and follow it slowly. You do not need to name every shade.

In the imagined walk, a breeze touches the branch. One leaf stirs, then another. You can picture the papery rustle arriving a little after the movement. The leaf you have been holding beside your sleeve belongs again among the others beside the path.

As you continue, a bright sound begins to gather beyond the trees: water moving over stones. Sunlight finds openings in the canopy. You follow those small glimmers toward Dunbar’s lively meeting with the season.

At the water’s edge, the afternoon becomes lively again. A little run of current slips between two stones and opens into a shallow pool. The sound is bright at the narrow place, softer where the water spreads. A leaf turns once on the surface, catches briefly against a twig, and moves on.

Dunbar’s speaker meets autumn with a spirited argument: the season can laugh, sing, and dress in splendid colors. Let the playfulness of his poem move beside the stream. There is room here for your own mood, too. If the day has felt heavy, you can sit with that heaviness while the water keeps its quick, uneven music. Delight can be something you encounter at the edge of your attention.

Watch one patch of light. It breaks into several pieces, draws together, and changes again. You might find yourself waiting for the next gleam. The poem’s cheerfulness has this kind of motion—something to spend a little time with, while the leaves and water continue on their way.

Let the imagined stream keep its soft movement while you look upward into this Seattle ginkgo. The photograph gathers sunlight in many little fans. Some are clear at their edges; others overlap until the yellow becomes a single bright place.

Picture sitting a little back from the water beneath such a canopy. The ground is cool, and your hands can rest easily. A leaf turns slowly as it falls. For a moment it seems to borrow both rhythms: the unhurried air and the quick water below.

When you are ready, the path rises gently. The stream grows quieter behind you, and the trees stand farther apart. Your attention opens from one yellow leaf to a whole tree carrying its autumn color. Jackson’s October waits in that wider view.

Beyond the trees, the sky feels very wide. Hills overlap in the distance, their wooded slopes carrying patches of yellow, brown, and lingering green. Close to the path, a grass stem bends and straightens. You have come from watching the smallest turns of water to a view the eye can wander across.

Helen Hunt Jackson’s October is lavish with color, and her sonnet also gives death a place within that splendor. Allow that sharper note to remain in the poem. Perhaps the hillside seems all the more vivid because its colors are passing. Perhaps, today, you simply want to look. Both ways of being here can have their space.

Choose one patch of gold on a distant slope. Let it hold your attention for a few breaths, as naturally as a sound might. Then take in the blue above it. The small detail and the larger view belong to the same afternoon. There is time to move gently between them.

Rest with the red tree in Seattle Japanese Garden. Around its bright crown, the photograph keeps the greens of shrubs and grass. Let your gaze move from the vivid center to those quieter edges, and back again when it wishes.

In the dreamed afternoon, a shadow lengthens across the path. The last sunlight catches a few leaves above you while the ground has already grown cool. You can carry Jackson’s sense of passing splendor without having to turn it into a lesson. For now, there is this color, and time to look.

The path bends toward an imagined place to rest. Ahead, a smaller glow takes the place of the sunlit tree. Let it become the warmth of Stevenson’s poem: a little fire in the gathering evening, held safely within the dream.

You reach a place to sit a comfortable distance from a small fire. In this imagined evening, the warmth comes and goes with the air. One side of a stone catches the glow; its other side is already the color of dusk. Farther away, the outlines of the hills become simple and dark.

Stevenson’s short poem follows the red of autumn fires and the rising trail of smoke. Summer’s flowers have finished, and his attention finds another kind of brightness. Let the short lines keep their plain, almost singing rhythm. After the wide hillside, there is something easy to follow in this small scene: a red center, a lifting flame, a gray strand loosening into the sky.

Sit with the changing light for a moment. Imagine the warmth reaching the backs of your hands, then a cooler breath of air passing over them. The scene can be as vivid or as faint as it comes. Here in the room, your body remains supported while the imagined evening gathers around the fire.

The photograph returns you to leaves. These red and green shapes were photographed in Seattle’s Discovery Park in 1983. The archive preserves their color; it does not tell us what anyone standing there felt. You can meet them with your own attention.

Notice how the red occupies only part of a leaf, and how the cooler background lets that red seem warmer. In your imagined resting place, the fire can settle to a small glow. You may keep the feeling of warmth at your hands while your eyes follow the uneven border between two colors.

There is only a short way left to go. Picture a table beneath a shelter, a basket set near its edge, the evening air moving through the open side. The leaves remain behind you as Blake’s harvest guest comes into the poem.

At the table, imagine drawing out a chair. The wood is worn smooth along its front edge. There are pears in the basket, and in this dreamed scene you might place a small bunch of grapes beside them, letting Blake’s opening image join the harvest.

In his poem, Autumn becomes a guest invited under a roof to rest and sing. This is Blake’s personification: a season given a voice and a departing figure. Follow it as you would the changing shape of a dream. The guest rises and goes over the hills; the harvest remains.

Set your hands near the basket. The fruit has the quiet particularity of things seen close up: one pear leans against another, a stem bends, a small mark interrupts the yellow skin. The journey began beside an apple still on its branch. Now it has brought you to fruit gathered and set down. Around this table, the day feels spacious enough to include both the guest’s leaving and what has been left to share.

Let the table and the basket stay within the dream. The final photograph opens onto a leaf-covered path in Discovery Park. There is green above it still, and gold scattered underfoot. The person walking there has a life beyond this single frame; you can leave their story open.

Imagine taking a few easy steps along a path of your own. Perhaps a detail from the journey comes with you: the golden branches in Portland, a fine red maple leaf, the little fans of a ginkgo, or a patch of green holding its place among the changing colors. The six poets have offered different ways of meeting autumn. Their voices can remain distinct as the walk comes to rest.

Begin to notice the surface actually supporting you. Feel where your hands are resting, or where your feet meet the floor. Let an ordinary sound nearby become clearer. If your eyes have been closed, open them when you wish; if they have stayed open, allow the room around the photograph to come forward.

There is time for one small movement: a shift of your fingers, a change of position, a look toward the window. The imagined path can recede at its own pace. You are here, with the rest of your day still ahead of you.
